Executive Summary The attached concept note frames this study as a mixed-methods inquiry into how organizational coaching contributes to leadership maturity and how its effects may extend across teams, organizational culture, business outcomes, stakeholder relationships, and the wider ecosystem. Rather than asking only whether coaching "works," the study seeks to understand how far coaching impact travels, through whom, and under what conditions it becomes sustainable. It also adopts an inclusive sampling approach that does not assume prior coaching exposure, distinguishing between structured coaching users, selective or past users, prospective users, non-users, and respondents who are unsure of their organization's coaching practices. This framing is well supported by the broader evidence base. Organizational coaching has consistently demonstrated positive effects at the individual level through peer-reviewed meta-analyses, including improvements in performance, skills, well-being, work attitudes, coping, and goal-directed self-regulation. A 2014 meta-analysis of 18 organizational studies reported positive effects across all five outcome categories, with effect sizes ranging from 0.43 to 0.74. A subsequent meta-analysis restricted to 37 randomized controlled trials also found a statistically significant moderate overall effect for workplace coaching. Additional meta-analytic evidence suggests particularly strong effects on behavioural change and relationship outcomes, making coaching especially relevant for a study that seeks to understand how its influence extends beyond individual leaders into broader organizational and ecosystem outcomes. Systematic reviews also caution that future coaching research requires stronger methodological rigor, greater contextual sensitivity, and more robust approaches to outcome measurement. The implications for questionnaire design are clear. This cannot be a generic HR survey or a simple "Did coaching work?" questionnaire. Instead, it must function as a consulting-grade organizational research instrument that combines measurement with intelligent routing. The instrument should distinguish between structured coaching users, selective or past users, prospective users, non-users, and respondents who are unsure of coaching exposure; capture both observable outcomes and barriers to adoption; and examine ripple effects across leadership, teams, organizations, stakeholders, ecosystems, and society. It should also incorporate branching logic, common organizational maturity measures, user-specific outcome measures, non-user readiness and barrier measures, and an embedded qualitative pathway through follow-up interviews and case studies. Accordingly, the proposed research instrument comprises a concise, executive-friendly online survey for CHROs and senior People Leaders, complemented by a semi-structured interview guide to provide qualitative depth. The primary respondent should ideally be one senior representative per organization, typically the CHRO, Chief People Officer, Head of Learning & Development, Head of Talent, or Organizational Development leader with enterprise-wide visibility. This approach is consistent with established organizational coaching research, including the ICF/HCI Coaching Culture studies, which similarly rely on HR, Talent, and Learning leaders to evaluate organizational coaching practices, access, integration, and impact. In practical terms, the questionnaire is intentionally designed to be methodologically neutral and analytically robust. It asks respondents about the extent to which coaching has contributed to outcomes rather than claiming it has caused them, includes response options such as "Not Sure," "Not Applicable," and "Too Early to Assess," and measures both perceived outcomes and organizational measurement practices rather than encouraging exaggerated claims. The wording remains concise, neutral, and free from leading or double-barrelled questions, enabling more reliable data collection across organizations with varying levels of coaching maturity. The resulting instrument is designed to be analytically rigorous, ethically appropriate, and practical for a senior HR audience. With an estimated completion time of 10–12 minutes, it supports descriptive and comparative statistics, reliability analysis, correlation, regression, and thematic qualitative analysis, fully aligned with the methodology proposed in the concept note. Research Logic and Hypothesis-To-Instrument Map The concept note’s central question is: How does organizational coaching build leadership maturity and create wider ripple effects across the organization and its ecosystem?  The framework is explicitly non-linear: coaching intervention may lead to self-awareness and clarity, behavioural change, leadership maturity, team and culture outcomes, and stakeholder/ecosystem impact, but the study must also identify where the chain weakens or stops.  The scope of inquiry spans individual leadership, teams, organizations, stakeholders, business ecosystems, and community/society. The survey and interview guide therefore need to measure both vertical depth and horizontal spread.  A rigorous instrument for this study should be built around a set of derived working hypothesis. These are not stated verbatim in the concept note, but they are the clearest testable interpretations of its framework. Derived working hypothesis What it means analytically Best respondent(s) Best instrument component  H1  Coaching maturity hypothesis  Organizations with more structured, embedded coaching practices will report stronger leadership-maturity outcomes than selective, informal, prospective, or non-user organizations. 
 CHRO, CLO, Head L&D  Survey + interview 
 H2  Integration hypothesis  The more coaching is integrated into talent, succession, transitions, and manager development, the stronger the reported team and organizational ripple effects. 
 CHRO, Head L&D, CLO  Survey + interview 
 H3  Measurement discipline hypothesis  Organizations that define metrics and review coaching outcomes more systematically will report stronger confidence in ROI and sustainability.  CHRO, CLO, Head L&D  Survey + interview 
 H4  Leadership-mediation hypothesis 
 Leadership-maturity outcomes are likely to mediate the relationship between coaching exposure and later team/organizational outcomes.  CHRO, Business Leader Survey 
 H5  Cross-boundary hypothesis  Ripple effects are stronger at the individual/team level than at the stakeholder/ecosystem level unless reinforced by culture, sponsor alignment, and systems integration. 
 CHRO, Business Leader  Survey + interview 
 H6  Barrier/readiness hypothesis  Non-users and prospective users will report distinct barriers, substitute interventions, and evidence needs that predict organizational readiness for future adoption. 
 CHRO, Head L&D  Survey + interview 
 H7  Context hypothesis  Industry, size, ownership, and region will shape both adoption patterns and the type of outcomes organizations report.  All segments  Survey 
 This hypothesis set fits both the concept note and the external research literature. Meta-analytic evidence supports asking about leadership-level and performance-related outcomes, including skills, affective outcomes, performance, work attitudes, coping, and self-regulation.  Research on leader coaching and managerial coaching also supports team learning, innovation effectiveness, task performance, organizational learning culture, and engagement as plausible downstream domains. At the same time, executive-coaching reviews emphasize the importance of methodological rigor and context sensitivity, which is why the questionnaire below distinguishes observable contribution from perceived potential and uses routing for users and non-users rather than blending them into a single outcome battery.

Respondent Architecture The respondent architecture should also be deliberate. The concept note names CHRO/CXO interviews and an organizational survey across varied coaching maturity levels.  • For organizational analysis, the primary survey respondent should ideally be the CHRO/CPO or equivalent People Leader.  • CLOs and Heads of L&D can complete the survey where they own coaching design and measurement.  • Business Leaders are most useful in the interview stream because they can comment on strategy execution, customer, partner, supplier, and ecosystem effects more directly than HR can.   Respondent segment Best used for Priority constructs CHRO / CPO / HR Head Primary organizational survey respondent  Coaching maturity, integration into talent systems, succession, retention, culture, governance, ROI confidence 
CLO / Chief Talent / Learning Head Survey or interview  Program design, modalities, target populations, internal/external coaching mix, measurement methods 
Head L&D / OD / Leadership Development Survey or interview  Adoption barriers, leadership outcomes, manager-as-coach, learning integration, readiness 
Business Leader / CXO sponsor Primarily interview  Team effects, execution, stakeholder outcomes, business value, where ripple strengthens or stops 
 From a question-design perspective, the instrument should follow established survey-writing principles: ask one concept at a time, keep wording short and understandable, avoid biasing terms, use logical ordering, and ensure response options are mutually exclusive and reasonably exhaustive, including neutral or “don’t know” options where appropriate.  Because this is an executive audience, the online survey should ideally be shown one question per page, with short prompts and light guidance only where needed.

Analysis design and codebook Recommended sample sizes and analytic approach The concept note targets approximately 150 organizations and explicitly includes both survey and qualitative inquiry. A practical design is to aim for 150 usable organizational survey responses, while ensuring one primary organizational record per organization. Where two respondents from the same organization reply, choose one primary respondent for organization-level analysis and retain the second as supplemental data for triangulation.  For interviews, a target of 24–32 interviews is strong enough to capture sector and maturity variation without becoming unwieldy. Why this survey design is methodologically strong This design is intentionally neutral. It uses “contributed to” rather than “caused,” avoids emotionally loaded terms such as “successful” or “transformational,” and includes “not sure,” “not applicable,” and “too early to assess” options. That avoid pushing respondents toward one side, to ask one concept at a time, and to use careful, exhaustive response options; it also respects the concept note’s explicit commitment to studying users, selective users, prospective users, non-users, and unsure respondents without assuming prior exposure.  The survey also maps cleanly onto the evidence base. Individual and organization-level items are justified by coaching meta-analyses on skills, affect, performance, work attitudes, coping, and self-regulation. Team and team-learning items are supported by research connecting leader coaching with team learning, innovation effectiveness, and task performance. The coaching-culture and measurement items reflect existing organizational coaching-culture research that emphasizes program design, manager coaching capability, internal coaching capability, and impact evaluation as hallmarks of stronger coaching cultures.
